NPLab · Year one · The common route

Minimum Theoretical

The shared theory every NPLab member can explain, use and criticise.

NPLab works across brains, animals, social systems and artificial agents with one toolkit: network science, higher-order networks and topology, with information theory as the common measure. This is the smallest body of theory that lets us read each other's work, ask useful questions, criticise results and move between projects. The name is a deliberate echo of Landau's Theoretical Minimum.

Draft curriculum. Readings and exercises will change after the first pilot.

How it works

Weeks 1–12 cover eight competencies. Each has foundation readings, one exercise and a checkpoint: you present your work at a Thursday lab meeting. Deepening and synthesis readings run alongside your project until the end of the first year. The provisional load is 8–10 hours a week; the first cohort will measure it.

Completion means you can:

  1. explain the central construction or argument;
  2. use it in a toy or project-relevant setting;
  3. identify its assumptions and common failure modes;
  4. distinguish descriptive structure from inference and mechanism;
  5. connect it to at least one NPLab research programme;
  6. state what evidence would weaken or falsify the associated claim.

Prefer to play? Quest mode follows the same route as a role-playing game. Your progress stays in your own browser.

C1

Week 1 · Foundation

Models, nulls, and scientific claims

Specify variables, scales, assumptions, observables, and a null or comparison model. Separate description, prediction, parameter inference, mechanism, and intervention. Identify leakage and circularity.

Exit evidenceDesign a matched null and state what result would weaken the claim.

Readings

Foundation

  • Fosdick, Larremore, Nishimura & Ugander (2018), Configuring random graph models with fixed degree sequences. SIAM Review.

    How to sample network null models correctly — the reference for graph nulls.

  • Gelman & Loken (2013), The garden of forking paths. (working paper / American Scientist).

    Researcher degrees of freedom and the multiple-comparisons trap in exploratory work.

  • Kapoor & Narayanan (2023), Leakage and the reproducibility crisis in machine-learning-based science. Patterns.

    Data leakage — the dominant failure mode in ML-for-science.

Deepening

  • Gelman, Carlin, Stern, Dunson, Vehtari & Rubin (2013), Bayesian Data Analysis (3rd ed.). CRC.

    First pass: Chapters 1, 5–7, and 10–11

    The practical reference. Hierarchical models, MCMC, model checking.

  • Jaynes (2003), Probability Theory: The Logic of Science. Cambridge.

    First pass: Chapters 1–4 and 11

    Philosophical foundation for Bayesian reasoning. Ch. 1–4, 11.

Exercise

Design and defend two competing null models for one lab result, and show which apparent effect each one destroys.

  1. Pick one result: yours, or a lab result you can rerun.
  2. State the claim in one sentence. Then write the dullest process that could have produced the same data.
  3. Build two null models and list the constraints each one preserves.
  4. Run both, and tabulate which apparent effects each null destroys.
  5. Map leakage and forks: what entered model selection, and how many analysis paths you tried.
  6. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableA two-page null-model dossier plus executable comparison of two null ensembles on a live or synthetic result.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing a null that preserves the wrong constraints, or allowing preprocessing and leakage to answer the question in advance.

How to guard against them

  • Say which graph space you sample (simple or multi-edge, with or without self-loops, stub- or vertex-labelled), then sample it correctly. See Fosdick, Larremore, Nishimura & Ugander (2018).
  • Split first. Fit every preprocessing and selection step inside the training fold. Run the leakage taxonomy as a checklist before you believe the score. See Kapoor & Narayanan (2023).
  • Write down the forks before you look, report how many you walked, and keep exploratory structure labelled as exploratory. See Gelman & Loken (2013).

The question to ask

What result would show that the reported structure is compatible with an uninteresting data-generating process?

CheckpointPresent your work at a Thursday lab meeting.

Beyond the common route
  • Marek et al. (2022), Reproducible brain-wide association studies require thousands of individuals. Nature. Research-stream reading (brain track).

C2

Weeks 2–3 · Foundation

Graph structure, operators, and spectra

Move between adjacency, incidence, Laplacian, random-walk, and spectral views. Explain which structural or dynamical question each representation answers.

Exit evidenceDerive or compute a Laplacian-based quantity on a small network and interpret it without overclaiming.

Readings

Foundation

  • Latora, Nicosia & Russo (2017), Complex Networks: Principles, Methods and Applications. Cambridge.

    First pass: Chapter route pending an edition check

    Best modern textbook. Priority: Ch. 1–6, 10.

  • Spielman (2025), Spectral and Algebraic Graph Theory (incomplete online draft dated 2 April 2025). https://www.cs.yale.edu/homes/spielman/sagt/

    First pass: Chapter route pending an edition check

    Laplacians, effective resistance, spectral clustering — the bridge to networks and renormalization.

Deepening

  • Peixoto (2023), Descriptive vs. inferential community detection in networks. Cambridge Elements.

    The principled network-inference position statement.

  • Molloy & Reed (1995), A critical point for random graphs with a given degree sequence. Random Structures & Algorithms.

    The Molloy–Reed criterion for the giant component.

  • Callaway, Newman, Strogatz & Watts (2000), Network robustness and fragility: percolation on random graphs. PRL.

    Robustness as percolation.

  • Albert, Jeong & Barabási (2000), Error and attack tolerance of complex networks. Nature.

    Landmark robustness paper.

Synthesis

  • Buldyrev, Parshani, Paul, Stanley & Havlin (2010), Catastrophic cascade of failures in interdependent networks. Nature.

    Landmark interdependent-networks paper.

Exercise

Describe one network three ways (descriptive, spectral and generative) and decide which account the data actually support.

  1. Pick a network you know, and write down the structural question you are asking of it.
  2. Move between the adjacency, Laplacian and random-walk views, and say which question each one answers.
  3. Compute one Laplacian quantity (spectral gap, Fiedler vector or a diffusion time) and interpret it without overclaiming.
  4. Find a partition descriptively, then fit a generative model and compare the two by description length.
  5. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableA model-comparison report contrasting descriptive, spectral, and generative accounts of one network.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a detected partition or higher-order reconstruction as evidence without comparing lower-order and latent-variable explanations.

How to guard against them

  • Fit a generative model, compare it against alternatives by description length, and accept a partition only if it beats them. See Peixoto (2023).

The question to ask

Which alternative generative model explains the same observations with equal or lower description length?

CheckpointPresent your work at a Thursday lab meeting.

Beyond the common route
  • Barabási & Albert (1999), Emergence of scaling in random networks. Science. Preferential attachment and scale-free networks.
  • Newman (2003), The structure and function of complex networks. SIAM Review. The survey that defined the field.
  • Watts & Strogatz (1998), Collective dynamics of 'small-world' networks. Nature. The small-world model. Short and elegant.

C3

Week 4 · Foundation

Higher-order representations

Distinguish hypergraphs, simplicial complexes, temporal/multilayer structures, and multivariate random variables. Higher order must be justified against a pairwise or latent-variable alternative.

Exit evidenceEncode the same toy system in two representations and show what information changes.

Readings

Foundation

  • Battiston, Cencetti, Iacopini, Latora, Lucas, Patania, Young & Petri (2020), Networks beyond pairwise interactions: structure and dynamics. Physics Reports.

    The manifesto. Defines the field.

  • Torres, Blevins, Bassett & Eliassi-Rad (2021), The why, how, and when of representations for complex systems. SIAM Review.

    When and why different representations are appropriate.

Deepening

  • Bianconi (2021), Higher-Order Networks: An Introduction to Simplicial Complexes. Cambridge.

    First pass: Chapters 1–3 and 6–8, plus Appendix B (Hodge decomposition)

    First dedicated textbook. Algebraic topology, Hodge theory, higher-order dynamics. Essential for higher-order network theory.

  • Bick, Gross, Harrington & Schaub (2023), What are higher-order networks? SIAM Review.

    When higher-order representations are justified vs. modelling choices.

Synthesis

  • Young, Petri & Peixoto (2021), Hypergraph reconstruction from network data. Communications Physics.

    Bayesian framework for inferring higher-order structure.

Exercise

Represent one dataset as a graph, a hypergraph and a simplicial complex, and separate what follows from the data from what follows from the representation.

  1. Pick a dataset of group observations: co-authorship, co-sponsorship, co-firing.
  2. Encode it as a graph, a hypergraph and a simplicial complex.
  3. Write a claim ledger with three columns: measured, encoded and inferred.
  4. Fit a pairwise or latent-variable model and check whether it reproduces the higher-order signature.
  5. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableThree representations of the same dataset with a claim ledger separating measured, encoded, and inferred structure.

Common mistakes

  • Inferring a genuine higher-order mechanism merely because observations arrive in groups or admit a higher-order encoding.

How to guard against them

  • Fit the pairwise and latent-variable alternatives explicitly. Claim higher order only for the signature they cannot reproduce. See Torres, Blevins, Bassett & Eliassi-Rad (2021).

The question to ask

Can an explicitly fitted pairwise or latent-variable model reproduce the alleged higher-order signature?

CheckpointPresent your work at a Thursday lab meeting.

Next: C4 · Dynamics →

C4

Weeks 5–6 · Foundation

Dynamics, stochasticity, and emergence

Reason about fixed points, stability, bifurcations, diffusion, contagion, synchronisation, fluctuations, phase transitions, and finite-size effects.

Exit evidenceAnalyse one dynamical model and distinguish a mathematical transition from an empirical universality claim.

Readings

Foundation

  • Strogatz (2015), Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos (2nd ed.). Westview Press (verify publisher of record).

    First pass: Chapters 1–8

    Best introductory text. Ch. 1–8.

  • Barrat, Barthélemy & Vespignani (2008), Dynamical Processes on Complex Networks. Cambridge.

    First pass: Chapters 4–7 and 9: dynamical processes, phase transitions, resilience and robustness, synchronization, epidemic spreading; plus the appendix on the generating-functions formalism (pp. 303–305)

    Essential companion to Newman. Ch. 7 (synchronization), 9 (epidemics), 10 (social dynamics).

  • Pastor-Satorras & Vespignani (2001), Epidemic spreading in scale-free networks. PRL.

    Absence of an epidemic threshold in scale-free networks.

Deepening

  • Sethna (2006), Statistical Mechanics: Entropy, Order Parameters, and Complexity. Oxford.

    First pass: Chapters 2, 7, 8, and 12

    Best modern stat-mech for complex systems. Freely available. Ch. 2, 7, 8, 12.

  • Centola & Macy (2007), Complex contagion and the weakness of long ties. American Journal of Sociology.

    Simple vs complex contagion.

  • Iacopini, Petri, Barrat & Latora (2019), Simplicial models of social contagion. Nature Communications.

    Group interactions create bistability and discontinuous transitions. Founding paper.

  • Newman (2002), Spread of epidemic disease on networks. Physical Review E.

    Maps SIR epidemics onto bond percolation.

  • Arenas, Díaz-Guilera, Kurths, Moreno & Zhou (2008), Synchronization in complex networks. Physics Reports.

    Comprehensive synchronization review.

Exercise

Simulate a bifurcation, a pairwise complex contagion and a higher-order contagion transition, and tell their mechanisms apart.

  1. Simulate a saddle-node or Hopf bifurcation and identify its order parameter.
  2. Simulate a pairwise complex contagion and a simplicial (higher-order) contagion.
  3. For each, name the mechanism, the order parameter and the finite-size effects.
  4. Run the finite-size or heterogeneity test that could make each transition disappear.
  5. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableA simulation study comparing three transition mechanisms, including finite-size and alternative-model checks.

Common mistakes

  • Treating abrupt change, scaling, or a power law as sufficient evidence of universality or criticality.

How to guard against them

  • Compare against alternative distributions, check finite-size scaling, and name the order parameter and the mechanism before using the word critical. See Sethna (2006).

The question to ask

Which finite-size, heterogeneity, or model-selection test would make the claimed transition disappear?

CheckpointPresent your work at a Thursday lab meeting.

Beyond the common route
  • Kuramoto (1984), Chemical Oscillations, Waves, and Turbulence. Springer. The synchronization reference.

Next: C6 · Topology →

C6

Week 7 · Foundation

Topology and persistent structure

Understand filtrations, homology, persistence, stability, and the relationship between topological summaries and the data-generating process.

Exit evidenceProduce and stress-test a persistence summary against a noise or surrogate baseline.

Readings

Foundation

  • Carlsson (2009), Topology and Data. Bulletin of the AMS.

    Launched TDA as a field. Start here for intuition.

Deepening

  • Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner & Harer (2007), Stability of persistence diagrams. Discrete & Computational Geometry.

    Persistence diagrams are stable under perturbation (bottleneck distance). The theorem that makes TDA trustworthy.

  • Edelsbrunner & Harer (2010), Computational Topology: An Introduction. AMS.

    First pass: Chapters 1–4 and 7

    The textbook. Ch. 1–4 (simplicial complexes) and Ch. 7 (persistence).

Exercise

Compute persistence under controlled perturbations, and separate stability from significance and from domain meaning.

  1. Compute a persistence diagram on a point cloud or network you know.
  2. Perturb the metric, the filtration and the sampling, and tabulate what changes.
  3. Build a surrogate or null model, and compute its diagram.
  4. Decide which features beat the surrogate, and write a one-page limits statement.
  5. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableA persistence analysis under metric, filtration, sampling, and null-model perturbations, with a one-page limits statement.

Common mistakes

  • Equating mathematical stability with significance, mechanism, uniqueness, or biological relevance.

How to guard against them

  • Race every feature against a surrogate built from the same sampling, and vary the filtration and metric before you interpret it. See Carlsson (2009).

The question to ask

Does the topological conclusion survive plausible filtration, metric, sampling, and null-model alternatives?

CheckpointPresent your work at a Thursday lab meeting.

Beyond the common route
  • Petri, Expert, Turkheimer, Carhart-Harris, Nutt, Hellyer & Vaccarino (2014), Homological scaffolds of brain functional networks. J. R. Soc. Interface. Research-stream reading (capstone case).

Next: C5 · Information →

C5

Weeks 8–9 · Foundation

Information, dependence, and compression

Use entropy, mutual and conditional information, rate–distortion, and the information bottleneck. Distinguish interaction order from statistical dependence, and redundancy/synergy measures from mechanisms.

Exit evidenceCompute an information quantity in a toy system and state the estimator and decomposition ambiguities.

Readings

Foundation

  • Cover & Thomas (2006), Elements of Information Theory. Wiley.

    First pass: Chapters 2, 4, and 10

    THE textbook. Priority: Ch. 2 (entropy), 4 (MI), 10 (rate-distortion).

  • Watanabe (1960), Information theoretical analysis of multivariate correlation. IBM Journal of Research and Development.

    Introduces total correlation (multi-information) — the conceptual root of multivariate dependency measures.

  • Rosas, Mediano, Gastpar & Jensen (2019), Quantifying high-order interdependencies via multivariate extensions of the mutual information. Physical Review E. decided 2026-10-07

    Introduced the O-information (signed synergy/redundancy).

Deepening

  • Tishby, Pereira & Bialek (2000), The Information Bottleneck Method. Proc. Allerton Conference on Communication, Control and Computing (verify year). arXiv:physics/0004057.

    Compression–relevance tradeoff. Theoretical ancestor of the semanticity programme.

  • Zaslavsky, Kemp, Regier & Tishby (2018), Efficient compression in color naming across languages. PNAS.

    IB explains cross-linguistic color-naming universals. Beautiful empirical validation.

  • Timme & Lapish (2018), A tutorial for information theory in neuroscience. eNeuro. decided 2026-10-07

    Practical guide to applying IT measures to neural data.

  • Williams & Beer (2010), Nonnegative decomposition of multivariate information. arXiv:1004.2515. decided 2026-10-07

    Foundational Partial Information Decomposition (PID).

Exercise

Measure compression, redundancy and synergy on systems whose mechanisms you built yourself, and find where the measures disagree.

  1. Derive or compute a toy rate–distortion curve.
  2. Change the distortion or the relevance variable, and say how the conclusion changes.
  3. Build XOR, COPY and common-drive systems whose mechanisms you know.
  4. Compute O-information and at least two redundancy measures on each, and mark where they disagree.
  5. Vary sample size and estimator, then write a one-paragraph non-uniqueness statement.
  6. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableA synthetic benchmark with known mechanisms, sample-size curves, estimator comparison, and a non-uniqueness statement.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the sign or magnitude of O-information or a PID atom as a unique physical mechanism.
  • Treating a convenient metric, distortion, or compression objective as if it were the system's intrinsic semantics.

How to guard against them

  • Swap the distortion or the relevance variable and check whether the conclusion survives the swap. See Tishby, Pereira & Bialek (2000).
  • Benchmark on systems with a known mechanism, compare two or more redundancy measures, and report sample-size curves. See Rosas, Mediano, Gastpar & Jensen (2019).

The question to ask

Can sampling bias, common drive, discretization, or another decomposition generate the same signature?

CheckpointPresent your work at a Thursday lab meeting.

C7

Weeks 10–11 · Foundation

Temporal and observational inference

Model sequences, hidden states, nonstationarity, and directed prediction. Understand why temporal order or predictive improvement alone does not prove a causal mechanism.

Exit evidenceCompare two temporal models and identify an observation or intervention that would discriminate them.

Readings

Foundation

  • Shumway & Stoffer (2017), Time Series Analysis and Its Applications (4th ed.). Springer.

    First pass: Chapters 1–4 and 6

    AR / state-space models and spectral analysis. The standard.

  • Pearl, Glymour & Jewell (2016), Causal Inference in Statistics: A Primer. Wiley. decided 2026-10-07

    The accessible entry point.

Deepening

  • Pearl (2009), Causality: Models, Reasoning, and Inference (2nd ed.). Cambridge.

    First pass: Chapters 1 and 3, Sections 6.1–6.2, and Chapter 7

    Causal graphs and do-calculus; the reference.

  • Schreiber & Schmitz (2000), Surrogate time series. Physica D.

    Surrogate-data null tests for nonlinearity and coupling.

  • Barnett, Barrett & Seth (2009), Granger causality and transfer entropy are equivalent for Gaussian variables. Physical Review Letters.

    The caveat to keep in mind when reading directed-information results.

  • Runge et al. (2019), Detecting and quantifying causal associations in large nonlinear time series datasets. Science Advances.

    Causal discovery in time series (PCMCI) — relevant to fMRI and codas.

Exercise

On synthetic data with a hidden confounder, show what extra assumptions or interventions would turn dependence into a causal claim.

  1. Generate a nonstationary sequential system with a hidden confounder.
  2. Draw the DAG, and use d-separation and backdoor adjustment to say what is identifiable.
  3. Fit a latent-state model (an HMM or state-space model) and run a surrogate-data test.
  4. Run Granger or transfer-entropy discovery, and show where it points the wrong way.
  5. Name the intervention that would settle the question.
  6. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableA checked probabilistic model plus a causal-claim ledger listing assumptions, identifiable effects, and non-identifiable alternatives.

Common mistakes

  • Treating posterior concentration, predictive success, Granger predictability, transfer entropy, or a fitted arrow as a causal mechanism.
  • Forcing stationarity, selecting hidden states post hoc, or interpreting directed predictability as an identified mechanism.

How to guard against them

  • Write the causal model. State which effects are identifiable, which confounders could reverse the arrow, and which intervention would decide it. See Pearl, Glymour & Jewell (2016).
  • Test for changepoints and nonstationarity, fix the number of states before fitting, and compare against surrogates. See Shumway & Stoffer (2017).

The question to ask

Which model misfit, latent confounder, intervention, or invariance failure would reverse the interpretation?

CheckpointPresent your work at a Thursday lab meeting.

Beyond the common route
  • Rabiner (1989), A tutorial on hidden Markov models and selected applications in speech recognition. Proceedings of the IEEE. Research-stream reading (CETI/sequence work).

C8

Week 12 · Foundation

Representation, coarse-graining, and preserved quantities

Explain dimensional reduction, optimal transport, coarse-graining, and renormalisation in terms of what is preserved, distorted, or made identifiable. Connect these choices to limits on generalisation and information processing.

Exit evidenceDefine a reduction map and test whether its claimed invariant is actually preserved.

Readings

Foundation

  • Villegas, Gili, Mulder, Caldarelli & Bianconi (2023), Laplacian renormalization group for heterogeneous networks. Nature Physics.

    Diffusion-based spectral coarse-graining. The key recent paper.

  • Wilson (1975), The renormalization group: critical phenomena and the Kondo problem. Reviews of Modern Physics.

    The foundational RG paper. (Also Tier 3: Renormalization.)

Deepening

  • Kriegeskorte, Mur & Bandettini (2008), Representational similarity analysis. Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience.

    The method for comparing representations across brains and models via dissimilarity matrices.

  • Peyré & Cuturi (2019), Computational Optimal Transport. Foundations and Trends in ML.

    First pass: Chapters 1–4, 8, and Sections 10.2 and 10.6

    The practical OT reference: algorithms, Sinkhorn, applications. Start here.

  • Murphy, Thibeault, Allard & Desrosiers (2024), Duality between predictability and reconstructability in complex systems. Nature Communications.

    Coarse-graining trades reconstructability for predictability — a causal-emergence signature.

Exercise

Coarse-grain one network two ways and test whether the quantity you promised to preserve actually survives.

  1. Pick a network and write down, before anything else, the quantity a good reduction should preserve.
  2. Coarse-grain it with two schemes, for example Laplacian renormalization and a spectral or community-based reduction.
  3. Compare how each preserves diffusion, prediction and higher-order structure.
  4. Compare two representations of one system with RSA, and test whether the geometry predicts an independent task limit.
  5. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableA two-scheme coarse-graining comparison with preregistered preservation criteria and a failure analysis.

Common mistakes

  • Selecting the preserved quantity after observing which coarse-graining looks most successful.
  • Equating decodability, geometric alignment, or compression with causal use, compositionality, or semantics.

How to guard against them

  • Preregister the preserved quantity, then report the observables that fail under the transformation. See Villegas, Gili, Mulder, Caldarelli & Bianconi (2023).
  • Pair every decoding result with an intervention or an independent behavioural limit, and set a competing explanation against it. See Kriegeskorte, Mur & Bandettini (2008).

The question to ask

Which dynamical or inferential observable fails under the proposed scale transformation?

CheckpointPresent your work at a Thursday lab meeting.

Beyond the common route
  • Barlow (1961), Possible principles underlying the transformation of sensory messages. In Sensory Communication (MIT Press). Research-stream reading (semanticity/brain).
  • Nurisso, Fernando, Deshpande, …, Cohen & Petri (2025), Bound by Semanticity. ICLR 2026. Research-stream reading (capstone case).
  • Dayan & Abbott (2001), Theoretical Neuroscience. MIT Press. Research-stream reading (brain track).
  • Nurisso, Morandini, Lucas, Vaccarino, Gili & Petri (2025), Higher-order Laplacian renormalization. Nature Physics. Research-stream reading (multiscale track).

Tier 2 · Months 4–8

Research Streams

Go deep in one stream and stay literate in the others: one orientation reading per stream you do not follow. Streams are where your project lives.

Streams: draft v0.2 (2026-10-09), reconciled with Tier 1 and awaiting review

T2

Research stream · from month 4

Higher-order networks

Higher-order network theory, dynamics, and reconstruction. Build, analyse, and infer higher-order network models, and show in each case whether a pairwise or latent-variable alternative would suffice.

Builds onC2 C3 C4 C1 C7 Assumes from Tier 1: Bianconi (2021); Bick, Gross, Harrington & Schaub (2023); Peixoto (2023); Sethna (2006); Centola & Macy (2007); Iacopini, Petri, Barrat & Latora (2019); Newman (2002); Arenas, Díaz-Guilera, Kurths, Moreno & Zhou (2008).

If this is not your stream, readBattiston et al. (2021), The physics of higher-order interactions in complex systems. Nature Physics.

Estimated load: about 60 hours of reading and 35 for the exercise, over months 4–8.

Required readings

  • Lim (2020), Hodge Laplacians on graphs. SIAM Review.

    Why: Hodge Laplacians are the operators under every argument we make about flows and dynamics on complexes. Learn them here, properly, once.

  • Schaub, Benson, Horn, Lippner & Jadbabaie (2020), Random walks on simplicial complexes and the normalized Hodge 1-Laplacian. SIAM Review.

    Why: Diffusion on complexes is the baseline that any claim about higher-order spreading or renormalisation departs from.

  • Peel, Peixoto & De Domenico (2022), Statistical inference links data and theory in network science. Nature Communications.

    Why: The inferential standard your reconstructions will be judged by.

  • Pastor-Satorras, Castellano, Van Mieghem & Vespignani (2015), Epidemic processes in complex networks. Reviews of Modern Physics.

    Why: The full review behind the Tier 1 epidemic-threshold readings. A higher-order contagion transition only means something against this pairwise theory.

  • Skardal & Arenas (2019), Abrupt desynchronization and extensive multistability in globally coupled oscillator simplexes. PRL.

    Why: Coupling order alone produces abrupt synchronisation. Read it beside Iacopini et al. 2019 and Arenas et al. 2008 from Tier 1.

  • Preti, De Francisci Morales, Iacopini & Petri (2024), Higher-order null models as a lens for social systems. Physical Review X.

    Why: Higher-order null models: how to ask whether a group effect is real. Read it against a pairwise null that preserves degree and group-size heterogeneity.

  • Petri & Barrat (2018), Simplicial activity driven model. Physical Review Letters.
