How to use this guide

NPLab works across brains, animals, social systems and artificial agents with one toolkit: network science, higher-order networks and topology, with information theory as the common measure. This is the smallest body of theory that lets us read each other's work, ask useful questions, criticise results and move between projects. The name is a deliberate echo of Landau's Theoretical Minimum.

Weeks 1–12 cover eight competencies. Each has foundation readings, one exercise and a checkpoint: you present your work at a Thursday lab meeting. Deepening and synthesis readings run alongside your project until the end of the first year. The provisional load is 8–10 hours a week; the first cohort will measure it.

Completion means you can:

  1. explain the central construction or argument;
  2. use it in a toy or project-relevant setting;
  3. identify its assumptions and common failure modes;
  4. distinguish descriptive structure from inference and mechanism;
  5. connect it to at least one area of NPLab research;
  6. state what evidence would weaken or falsify the associated claim.

Prefer to play? Quest mode follows the same route as a role-playing game. Your progress stays in your own browser.

Specify variables, scales, assumptions, observables, and a null or comparison model. Separate description, prediction, parameter inference, mechanism, and intervention. Identify leakage and circularity.

Exit evidenceDesign a matched null and state what result would weaken the claim.

Readings

Foundation

  • Fosdick, Larremore, Nishimura & Ugander (2018), Configuring random graph models with fixed degree sequences. SIAM Review.

    How to sample network null models correctly — the reference for graph nulls.

  • Gelman & Loken (2013), The garden of forking paths. (working paper / American Scientist).

    Researcher degrees of freedom and the multiple-comparisons trap in exploratory work.

  • Kapoor & Narayanan (2023), Leakage and the reproducibility crisis in machine-learning-based science. Patterns.

    Data leakage — the dominant failure mode in ML-for-science.

Deepening

  • Gelman, Carlin, Stern, Dunson, Vehtari & Rubin (2013), Bayesian Data Analysis (3rd ed.). CRC.

    First pass: Chapters 1, 5–7, and 10–11

    The practical reference. Hierarchical models, MCMC, model checking.

  • Jaynes (2003), Probability Theory: The Logic of Science. Cambridge.

    First pass: Chapters 1–4 and 11

    Philosophical foundation for Bayesian reasoning. Ch. 1–4, 11.

Exercise

Design and defend two competing null models for one lab result, and show which apparent effect each one destroys.

  1. Pick one result: yours, or a lab result you can rerun.
  2. State the claim in one sentence. Then write the dullest process that could have produced the same data.
  3. Build two null models and list the constraints each one preserves.
  4. Run both, and tabulate which apparent effects each null destroys.
  5. Map leakage and forks: what entered model selection, and how many analysis paths you tried.
  6. In code: script both nulls so they rerun from the raw data with a fixed seed, and report how many samples you drew.
  7. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableA two-page null-model dossier plus executable comparison of two null ensembles on a live or synthetic result.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing a null that preserves the wrong constraints, or allowing preprocessing and leakage to answer the question in advance.
How to guard against them
  • Say which graph space you sample (simple or multi-edge, with or without self-loops, stub- or vertex-labelled), then sample it correctly. See Fosdick, Larremore, Nishimura & Ugander (2018).
  • Split first. Fit every preprocessing and selection step inside the training fold. Run the leakage taxonomy as a checklist before you believe the score. See Kapoor & Narayanan (2023).
  • Write down the forks before you look, report how many you walked, and keep exploratory structure labelled as exploratory. See Gelman & Loken (2013).

The question to ask

What result would show that the reported structure is compatible with an uninteresting data-generating process?

CheckpointPresent your work at a Thursday lab meeting.

Exercise

Describe one network three ways (descriptive, spectral and generative) and decide which account the data actually support.

  1. Pick a network you know, and write down the structural question you are asking of it.
  2. Move between the adjacency, Laplacian and random-walk views, and say which question each one answers.
  3. Compute one Laplacian quantity (spectral gap, Fiedler vector or a diffusion time) and interpret it without overclaiming.
  4. Find a partition descriptively, then fit a generative model and compare the two by description length.
  5. In code: on a real network of at least a few thousand nodes, fit a stochastic block model with graph-tool and compare it with modularity. Use sparse matrices, never a dense eigendecomposition.
  6. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableA model-comparison report contrasting descriptive, spectral, and generative accounts of one network.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a detected partition or higher-order reconstruction as evidence without comparing lower-order and latent-variable explanations.
How to guard against them
  • Fit a generative model, compare it against alternatives by description length, and accept a partition only if it beats them. See Peixoto (2023).

The question to ask

Which alternative generative model explains the same observations with equal or lower description length?

CheckpointPresent your work at a Thursday lab meeting.

    First dedicated textbook. Algebraic topology, Hodge theory, higher-order dynamics. Essential for higher-order network theory.

    When higher-order representations are justified vs. modelling choices.

    The formalism for networks with several kinds of edges or layers: when a multilayer description is needed, and what aggregating the layers throws away.

  • Holme & Saramäki (2012), Temporal networks. Physics Reports 519, 97–125.

    Edges that exist only at certain times: time-respecting paths, burstiness, and why aggregating over time changes what can spread.

Synthesis

  • Young, Petri & Peixoto (2021), Hypergraph reconstruction from network data. Communications Physics.

    Bayesian framework for inferring higher-order structure.

Exercise

Represent one dataset as a graph, a hypergraph and a simplicial complex, and separate what follows from the data from what follows from the representation.

  1. Pick a dataset of group observations: co-authorship, co-sponsorship, co-firing.
  2. Encode it as a graph, a hypergraph and a simplicial complex.
  3. Write a claim ledger with three columns: measured, encoded and inferred.
  4. Fit a pairwise or latent-variable model and check whether it reproduces the higher-order signature.
  5. In code: build the hypergraph and the simplicial complex with XGI, and compute one quantity that the pairwise projection cannot reproduce.
  6. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableThree representations of the same dataset with a claim ledger separating measured, encoded, and inferred structure.

Common mistakes

  • Inferring a genuine higher-order mechanism merely because observations arrive in groups or admit a higher-order encoding.
How to guard against them
  • Fit the pairwise and latent-variable alternatives explicitly. Claim higher order only for the signature they cannot reproduce. See Torres, Blevins, Bassett & Eliassi-Rad (2021).

The question to ask

Can an explicitly fitted pairwise or latent-variable model reproduce the alleged higher-order signature?

CheckpointPresent your work at a Thursday lab meeting.

Exercise

Simulate a bifurcation, a pairwise complex contagion and a higher-order contagion transition, and tell their mechanisms apart.

  1. Simulate a saddle-node or Hopf bifurcation and identify its order parameter.
  2. Simulate a pairwise complex contagion and a simplicial (higher-order) contagion.
  3. For each, name the mechanism, the order parameter and the finite-size effects.
  4. Run the finite-size or heterogeneity test that could make each transition disappear.
  5. In code: run the contagions on a real contact or co-membership network (XGI handles the higher-order case), with enough runs and sizes to show the finite-size effect.
  6. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableA simulation study comparing three transition mechanisms, including finite-size and alternative-model checks.

Common mistakes

  • Treating abrupt change, scaling, or a power law as sufficient evidence of universality or criticality.
How to guard against them
  • Compare against alternative distributions, check finite-size scaling, and name the order parameter and the mechanism before using the word critical. See Sethna (2006).

The question to ask

Which finite-size, heterogeneity, or model-selection test would make the claimed transition disappear?

Exercise

Compute persistence under controlled perturbations, and separate stability from significance and from domain meaning.

  1. Compute a persistence diagram on a point cloud or network you know.
  2. Perturb the metric, the filtration and the sampling, and tabulate what changes.
  3. Build a surrogate or null model, and compute its diagram.
  4. Decide which features beat the surrogate, and write a one-page limits statement.
  5. In code: compute the diagrams with GUDHI or Ripser, then repeat the surrogate test on recorded neural population activity, as Gardner et al. 2022 do for grid cells.
  6. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableA persistence analysis under metric, filtration, sampling, and null-model perturbations, with a one-page limits statement.

Common mistakes

  • Equating mathematical stability with significance, mechanism, uniqueness, or biological relevance.
How to guard against them
  • Race every feature against a surrogate built from the same sampling, and vary the filtration and metric before you interpret it. See Carlsson (2009).

The question to ask

Does the topological conclusion survive plausible filtration, metric, sampling, and null-model alternatives?

    THE textbook. Priority: Ch. 2 (entropy), 4 (MI), 10 (rate-distortion).

    Introduced the O-information (signed synergy/redundancy).

Deepening

  • Tishby, Pereira & Bialek (2000), The Information Bottleneck Method. Proc. Allerton Conference on Communication, Control and Computing (verify year). arXiv:physics/0004057.

    Compression–relevance tradeoff. Theoretical ancestor of the semanticity framework.

    IB explains cross-linguistic color-naming universals. Beautiful empirical validation.

  • Timme & Lapish (2018), A tutorial for information theory in neuroscience. eNeuro. decided 2026-10-07

    Practical guide to applying IT measures to neural data.

  • Williams & Beer (2010), Nonnegative decomposition of multivariate information. arXiv:1004.2515. decided 2026-10-07

    Foundational Partial Information Decomposition (PID).

Exercise

Measure compression, redundancy and synergy on systems whose mechanisms you built yourself, and find where the measures disagree.

  1. Derive or compute a toy rate–distortion curve.
  2. Change the distortion or the relevance variable, and say how the conclusion changes.
  3. Build XOR, COPY and common-drive systems whose mechanisms you know.
  4. Compute O-information and at least two redundancy measures on each, and mark where they disagree.
  5. Vary sample size and estimator, then write a one-paragraph non-uniqueness statement.
  6. In code: compute the toy measures with dit, then estimate O-information on one recorded dataset (spikes, imaging or behaviour) with two estimators, for example in JIDT or IDTxl.
  7. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableA synthetic benchmark with known mechanisms, sample-size curves, estimator comparison, and a non-uniqueness statement.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the sign or magnitude of O-information or a PID atom as a unique physical mechanism.
  • Treating a convenient metric, distortion, or compression objective as if it were the system's intrinsic semantics.
How to guard against them
  • Swap the distortion or the relevance variable and check whether the conclusion survives the swap. See Tishby, Pereira & Bialek (2000).
  • Benchmark on systems with a known mechanism, compare two or more redundancy measures, and report sample-size curves. See Rosas, Mediano, Gastpar & Jensen (2019).

The question to ask

Can sampling bias, common drive, discretization, or another decomposition generate the same signature?

Exercise

On synthetic data with a hidden confounder, show what extra assumptions or interventions would turn dependence into a causal claim.

  1. Generate a nonstationary sequential system with a hidden confounder.
  2. Draw the DAG, and use d-separation and backdoor adjustment to say what is identifiable.
  3. Fit a latent-state model (an HMM or state-space model) and run a surrogate-data test.
  4. Run Granger or transfer-entropy discovery, and show where it points the wrong way.
  5. Name the intervention that would settle the question.
  6. In code: run transfer entropy and its surrogate test with IDTxl or JIDT, on your synthetic system and on one real recording.
  7. Write down your answer to the falsification question.

DeliverableA checked probabilistic model plus a causal-claim ledger listing assumptions, identifiable effects, and non-identifiable alternatives.

Common mistakes

  • Treating posterior concentration, predictive success, Granger predictability, transfer entropy, or a fitted arrow as a causal mechanism.
  • Forcing stationarity, selecting hidden states post hoc, or interpreting directed predictability as an identified mechanism.
How to guard against them
  • Write the causal model. State which effects are identifiable, which confounders could reverse the arrow, and which intervention would decide it. See Pearl, Glymour & Jewell (2016).
  • Test for changepoints and nonstationarity, fix the number of states before fitting, and compare against surrogates. See Shumway & Stoffer (2017).

The question to ask

Which model misfit, latent confounder, intervention, or invariance failure would reverse the interpretation?

Synergy, irreducible dependence and topological scaffolds separate people, states and patients. Do they carry information that pairwise connectivity does not, once reliability and confounds are matched?

What is unsettled. Many higher-order measures are computed from the same signals as pairwise connectivity, so whether they add information or redescribe it is not settled. A more reliable measure also looks more distinctive, and more heritable, whatever it encodes. And pairwise maximum-entropy models already reproduce much of the structure of small neural populations.

The lab claim to testInstantaneous higher-order co-fluctuations separate dynamical regimes that pairwise statistics cannot, and leave measurable signatures in brain activity. Santoro, Battiston, Petri & Amico (2023), Higher-order organization of multivariate time series. Nature Physics.

Readings past the stream

  • Rival account Schneidman, Berry, Segev & Bialek (2006), Weak pairwise correlations imply strongly correlated network states in a neural population. Nature 440, 1007–1012.

    Why: The strongest competing account: pairwise models capture most of a population's correlated states, so higher order has to earn its place.

  • Ganmor, Segev & Schneidman (2011), Sparse low-order interaction network underlies a highly correlated and learnable neural population code. PNAS 108, 9679–9684.

    Why: Where pairs stop being enough: larger populations need a sparse set of low-order interactions.

  • Finn, Shen, Scheinost, Rosenberg, Huang, Chun, Papademetris & Constable (2015), Functional connectome fingerprinting: identifying individuals using patterns of brain connectivity. Nature Neuroscience 18, 1664–1671.

    Why: The pairwise fingerprinting baseline that a higher-order fingerprint has to beat.

  • Noble, Scheinost & Constable (2019), A decade of test-retest reliability of functional connectivity: a systematic review and meta-analysis. NeuroImage 203, 116157.

    Why: Reliability limits every comparison: a measure that is merely more reliable will look more distinctive.

CapstoneTake one published higher-order brain result and test whether pairwise connectivity with matched reliability, or a pairwise maximum-entropy model, reproduces it.

T3Open question · 4 readingsWhere do capacity limits come from?

Builds on Cognition, semanticity and NeuroAI

Generalization needs shared representations, and sharing causes interference. Does that cost, under finite precision, explain the capacity limits seen in people, animals and networks better than accounts based on resources or slots?

What is unsettled. The semantic-horizon account predicts a non-monotonic generalization curve and a ceiling of a few bits. Resource models, in which precision is spread more thinly as load grows, slot models with a fixed number of items, and control accounts based on interference each fit parts of the same data. Which observation separates them, and whether precision itself falls with load, is open.

The lab claim to testCapacity limits reflect the curse of generalization: the shared representations that support generalization also cause interference, and with any decision noise identification is capped at a few bits. Frankland, Marjieh, Nurisso et al. (2025), Capacity limits in cognitive processing reflect the curse of generalization. Psychological Review (accepted, 2026).

Readings past the stream

  • Rival account Ma, Husain & Bays (2014), Changing concepts of working memory. Nature Neuroscience 17, 347–356.

    Why: The strongest competing account: no fixed limit, only a resource spread across items.

  • Cowan (2001), The magical number 4 in short-term memory: a reconsideration of mental storage capacity. Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24, 87–114.

    Why: The case for a fixed item limit, and the evidence a ceiling of a few bits has to reproduce.

  • Sims (2018), Efficient coding explains the universal law of generalization in human perception. Science 360, 652–656.

    Why: Shepard's law from efficient coding: the nearest rival derivation of the generalization gradient.

  • Musslick & Cohen (2021), Rationalizing constraints on the capacity for cognitive control. Trends in Cognitive Sciences 25, 757–775.

    Why: Interference between shared representations as the reason control is limited: the account closest to ours.

CapstoneFit the semantic-horizon model, a resource model and a slot model to one capacity dataset with matched free parameters, and name the observation that separates them.

T3Open question · 5 readingsDoes representational geometry predict where models fail?

Builds on Cognition, semanticity and NeuroAI

In language and vision–language models, does the geometry of internal representations predict where binding, composition and generalization fail, and do models align with brains for the same reasons?

What is unsettled. Concept directions can be found and steered, but superposition and non-linear features complicate a purely linear picture. Convergence across models may reflect shared data rather than shared computation. And high alignment with brain data does not show that a model computes the way the brain does.

The lab claim to testIn vision–language models, the geometric overlap between concept vectors predicts error patterns such as hallucinated objects and confused similarity judgements. Savietto, Campbell, Panisson, Nurisso, Petri, Cohen & Perotti (2026), The geometry of representational failures in vision-language models. ICML 2026 (accepted). arXiv:2602.07025.

Readings past the stream

  • Rival account Bowers, Malhotra, Dujmović, Llera Montero, Tsvetkov, Biscione, Puebla, Adolfi, Hummel, Heaton, Evans, Mitchell & Blything (2023), Deep problems with neural network models of human vision. Behavioral and Brain Sciences 46, e385.

    Why: The strongest competing account: success on benchmarks and on brain prediction says little about whether a model works like the brain.

  • Park, Choe & Veitch (2024), The linear representation hypothesis and the geometry of large language models. ICML 2024. arXiv:2311.03658

    Why: What it means, formally, for a concept to be a direction, and which geometry that implies.

  • Campbell, Rane, Giallanza, De Sabbata, Ghods, Joshi, Ku, Frankland, Griffiths, Cohen & Webb (2024), Understanding the limits of vision language models through the lens of the binding problem. NeurIPS 2024. arXiv:2411.00238

    Why: The behavioural side: multi-object failures in vision–language models behave like the binding problem.

  • Chung, Lee & Sompolinsky (2018), Classification and geometry of general perceptual manifolds. Physical Review X.

    Why: Links the geometry of representations to how many categories a linear readout can separate.

  • Huh, Cheung, Wang & Isola (2024), Position: The Platonic representation hypothesis. ICML 2024. arXiv:2405.07987

    Why: The claim that models converge on one representation, and what would have to be true for that to mean shared computation.

CapstoneTake one published claim that a model represents a concept, and test whether the same geometry appears in a model that fails the behavioural test.

T3Open question · 4 readingsWhat carries information in sperm-whale codas?

Builds on Social, collective and animal communication · Animal communication

Codas vary in rhythm, tempo and ornamentation, and with context. Which of these variations carry information for the whales, and what evidence would show it?

What is unsettled. Combinatorial structure in codas is documented, but whether the whales use it compositionally, as identity and clan markers, or as a reflection of context is open. Repertoire claims depend on how codas are detected and annotated, and without playback experiments meaning can only be inferred from context.

The lab claim to testSperm-whale communication can be decoded by combining large, context-rich recordings with machine learning, then testing hypotheses with playback. Andreas, Beguš, Bronstein, Diamant, Delaney et al. (2022), Toward understanding the communication in sperm whales. iScience.

Readings past the stream

  • Rival account Hersh et al. (2022), Evidence from sperm whale clans of symbolic marking in non-human cultures. PNAS 119, e2201692119.

    Why: The strongest competing account: coda variation marks identity and clan, which is information, but not a message.

  • Sainburg, Thielk & Gentner (2020), Finding, visualizing, and quantifying latent structure across diverse animal vocal repertoires. PLoS Computational Biology.

    Why: How latent structure is found in vocal repertoires, and what the method assumes.

  • Arnon, Kirby, Allen, Garrigue, Carroll & Garland (2025), Whale song shows language-like statistical structure. Science 387, 649–653.

    Why: Language-like statistics in humpback song, and why statistics alone do not show meaning.

  • Hauser, Chomsky & Fitch (2002), The faculty of language: what is it, who has it, and how did it evolve? Science.

    Why: The bar a claim of language has to clear, as its sceptics set it.

CapstoneTake one published claim about coda structure and test it against a null that preserves individual and clan identity cues; name the observation, or the playback, that would show the whales use that structure.

T3Open question · 4 readingsDo real contagions need groups?

Builds on Social, collective and animal communication · Social coordination

Group reinforcement predicts sudden transitions and hysteresis in the spread of behaviour. Can observational data show that a real contagion is higher-order, rather than pairwise and complex, or driven by homophily?

What is unsettled. In observational data, complex contagion, homophily and shared exposure leave similar traces, and few experiments manipulate group structure directly. Methods that tell simple from complex contagion exist; whether they can identify group reinforcement is open.

The lab claim to testGroup reinforcement makes social contagion bistable, with discontinuous transitions that pairwise models do not produce. Iacopini, Petri, Barrat & Latora (2019), Simplicial models of social contagion. Nature Communications.

Readings past the stream

  • Rival account Shalizi & Thomas (2011), Homophily and contagion are generically confounded in observational social network studies. Sociological Methods & Research 40, 211–239.

    Why: The strongest competing account: in observational data, homophily can mimic contagion, so a spreading pattern alone proves little.

  • Centola (2010), The spread of behavior in an online social network experiment. Science 329, 1194–1197.

    Why: The experimental evidence for complex contagion, and what it does and does not show about groups.

  • Cencetti, Contreras, Mancastroppa & Barrat (2023), Distinguishing simple and complex contagion processes on networks. Physical Review Letters 130, 247401.

    Why: A test that tells simple from complex contagion from the infection sequence: a starting point for testing group reinforcement.

  • St-Onge, Iacopini, Latora, Barrat, Petri, Allard & Hébert-Dufresne (2022), Influential groups for seeding and sustaining nonlinear contagion in heterogeneous hypergraphs. Communications Physics 5, 25.

    Why: Lab paper: which groups seed and sustain nonlinear contagion, and so where to look in data.

CapstoneTake one dataset of adoptions or spreading behaviour, fit simple, pairwise complex and group contagion models, and show which the data can tell apart once homophily is controlled.

T3Open question · 4 readingsIs there a privileged scale?

Builds on Renormalization and criticality

Coarse-graining can make dynamics more predictable while making structure harder to reconstruct. Is there a scale at which a system is best described, and does it agree across dynamics, information and structure?

What is unsettled. Causal-emergence measures pick out a macroscale, but which one depends on how states are grouped and on the measure. Renormalization schemes preserve different things (diffusion, geometry, spectra) and can disagree about the scale. Whether a dynamics-preserving map exists at a given scale can be checked only for some schemes.

The lab claim to testHarmonic morphisms are the minimal condition under which random walks on a network project exactly onto random walks on its coarse-grained image, and the harmonic degree measures how close any coarse-graining comes. Guadagnuolo, Nurisso, Galluzzi, Allard & Petri (2026), Harmonic morphisms and dynamical invariants in network renormalization. arXiv:2604.08386 (under review, PRX).

Readings past the stream

  • Rival account Eberhardt & Lee (2022), Causal emergence: when distortions in a map obscure the territory. Philosophies 7, 30.

    Why: The strongest competing account: a measure that finds a better macroscale may be rewarding a distorted map.

  • Hoel, Albantakis & Tononi (2013), Quantifying causal emergence shows that macro can beat micro. PNAS.

    Why: The original claim that a macroscale can carry more causal information than the microscale.

  • Rosas, Mediano, Jensen, Seth, Barrett, Carhart-Harris & Bor (2020), Reconciling emergences: an information-theoretic approach to identify causal emergence in multivariate data. PLOS Computational Biology 16, e1008289.

    Why: A criterion for emergence that works on data, not only on known models.

  • Gfeller & De Los Rios (2007), Spectral coarse graining of complex networks. Physical Review Letters 99, 038701.

    Why: Coarse-graining that preserves the random-walk spectrum: the older baseline for dynamics-preserving reduction.

CapstoneTake one network, compute a causal-emergence measure along two renormalization flows, and show whether the best scale survives a change of scheme or of measure.

T3Open question · 4 readingsIs branching one principle or many?

Builds on Renormalization and criticality

Lungs, vessels, fungi, rivers and neural arbours all branch. Is there a shared principle, such as optimal transport, local growth rules or criticality, or only a family resemblance?

What is unsettled. Optimal-transport accounts predict scaling laws and loops, local growth rules produce branched forms without any optimisation, and adaptive feedback can retune a network after it grows. A branching process in time is also a different object from a branched network in space. Which measurement tells these accounts apart in a given system is open.

The lab claim to testStochastic branching, fusion and stopping, with no global optimisation or feedback, produce networks whose trade-offs between transport, exploration and robustness lie close to those of real fungal networks. Lucas, Bisot, Petri, Declerck & Carletti (2026), Minimal branching and fusion morphogenesis approaches biological multi-objective optimality. arXiv:2601.03877

Readings past the stream

  • Rival account Tero, Takagi, Saigusa, Ito, Bebber, Fricker, Yumiki, Kobayashi & Nakagaki (2010), Rules for biologically inspired adaptive network design. Science 327, 439–442.

    Why: The strongest competing account: efficient networks from flow-driven feedback, not from growth rules alone.

  • West, Brown & Enquist (1997), A general model for the origin of allometric scaling laws in biology. Science 276, 122–126.

    Why: The optimal-transport account of biological scaling, and the predictions it makes.

  • Katifori, Szöllősi & Magnasco (2010), Damage and fluctuations induce loops in optimal transport networks. Physical Review Letters 104, 048704.

    Why: Why optimal networks sometimes have loops: damage and fluctuating load change the optimum.

  • Hannezo, Scheele, Moad, Drogo, Heer, Sampogna, van Rheenen & Simons (2017), A unifying theory of branching morphogenesis. Cell 171, 242–255.

    Why: Branching organs from local stochastic rules: the growth account in development.

CapstoneTake one branching system with data, fit an optimal-transport model and a local growth model, and name the measurement that separates them.
